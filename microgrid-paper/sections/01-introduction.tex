\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Battery energy storage systems in distribution-network microgrids enable temporal arbitrage between renewable generation, load demand, and electricity pricing through sequential dispatch decisions. Each charge or discharge action propagates through state-of-charge dynamics, electrochemical losses, network constraints, and future operational flexibility\cite{nakabi2021segan,zhang2023multiagentECM,monfaredi2023multiagent}. While classical optimization excels under accurate models and forecasts, the proliferation of stochastic renewables, flexible loads, and distributed storage has motivated learning-based approaches that adapt control policies from operational data\cite{wang2023collaborative,lin2024coordinated,li2024deep,raj2024reinforcement,li2025optimizing,omar2025hierarchical,zhang2026safeTCE}. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has consequently emerged as a compelling framework, demonstrating economic viability across multi-agent coordination\cite{XU2023106774,zhang2024energy}, degradation-aware scheduling\cite{li2025battery,park2026worldmodel}, and resilient operation\cite{delavari2024rlRobust,momen2024resilience,amiri2026explainableECM}.

However, translating DRL algorithms into operational controllers confronts a fundamental evaluation gap: \emph{cumulative economic reward does not uniquely determine operational acceptability}. A low-cost trajectory may systematically park the battery near state-of-charge boundaries, suppress responsive cycling, or achieve constraint satisfaction primarily through external safety-filter corrections rather than internalized policy learning. For distribution networks where battery dispatch directly affects voltage profiles, loading limits, and grid reliability, controller selection requires trajectory-level operational audits alongside scalar performance metrics---a requirement inadequately addressed by existing paradigms prioritizing aggregate return alone\cite{henderson2018deepRLmatters,agarwal2021statistical}.

This evaluation challenge intersects critically with safe reinforcement learning. Battery storage inherently operates under hard physical constraints (state-of-charge bounds, power limits, network feasibility) and soft operational preferences (mid-band inventory maintenance, price-responsive behavior, terminal convergence). The safe DRL literature has developed constrained Markov decision process formulations\cite{achiam2017cpo}, control-barrier certificates\cite{brunke2022safeLearning}, model-predictive safety layers\cite{odriozola2023shielded}, and action-shielding mechanisms\cite{zhou2023safeRegion,mazumdar2024stopping,misra2024psafe,wang2025shieldedPlanning}. For power systems, safe DRL has demonstrated value in Volt-VAR control\cite{wang2020safeVoltVar}, distribution network operation\cite{li2022safeDistribution}, voltage stability\cite{bi2024safeVoltage}, and inverter regulation\cite{shuai2024safeInverter,omar2025interiorPoint,liu2026digitalTwin}. Yet these safety mechanisms introduce a secondary evaluation problem: \emph{when a deployed policy achieves acceptable performance primarily through shield corrections, measured outcomes overstate the learned policy's autonomous capability}---a distinction profoundly impacting engineering trust, failure-mode analysis, and regulatory acceptance.

For battery storage, this distinction amplifies because state-of-charge trajectory itself constitutes an operational observable. A policy may achieve favorable cost while chronically dwelling at inventory extremes, missing terminal targets, maintaining insufficient discharge reserve during peak-price periods, or exhibiting charge-discharge patterns inconsistent with intertemporal value gradients. Such behaviors are not fully captured by feasibility flags or cumulative metrics; they require trajectory-oriented diagnostics. We define \emph{battery morphology} as the ensemble of trajectory-level attributes---mid-band residence, boundary avoidance, terminal consistency, price-responsive directionality, infeasible-action suppression, network constraint satisfaction, and intervention diagnostics---that collectively determine whether a learned policy exhibits interpretable, operationally sound energy-shifting behavior versus merely producing acceptable cost after protective intervention.

This necessity aligns with broader recognition that algorithmic evaluation extends beyond aggregate performance metrics\cite{henderson2018deepRLmatters,agarwal2021statistical}. Studies of DRL reproducibility, transfer learning\cite{padakandla2021dynamicRL,zhu2023transferRL}, verification\cite{landers2023verification}, and explainability\cite{vouros2022xrl,milani2024xrl} emphasize that sequential decision systems demand assessment of robustness, interpretability, and behavioral patterns---not solely scalar returns. Applied to storage control, this principle mandates evaluation frameworks that reveal whether a controller has internalized meaningful energy arbitrage logic or merely produces acceptable outcomes through heavy reliance on corrective safety layers.

\textbf{Contributions.} Motivated by this operational validation gap, we present a morphology-aware validation framework for SAIL-SAC (Shield-Aware Intervention Learning with Soft Actor-Critic) in distribution-network microgrids. The framework integrates three components: (1) a shielded replay SAC backbone with Inventory Teacher safety filters that enforce feasibility while recording intervention magnitudes; (2) a multi-gate validation protocol (P4 BalancedGate) assessing policies across six morphological dimensions---mid-band dwelling, price-responsive directionality, infeasible-action suppression, boundary avoidance, terminal convergence, and intervention diagnostics; and (3) cross-fidelity testing spanning simplified efficiency-based battery models to detailed physics-based electrochemical representations, verifying policy robustness across model fidelities unavailable during training.

We evaluate the framework on an IEEE 33-bus distribution network stress case across 16~random seeds, identifying six successful seeds (37.5\% success rate) satisfying morphological gates: mid-band dwelling $>5\%$, infeasible actions $<30\%$. These policies achieve mean objective cost $11.788 \pm 0.120$\,M\textyen{} with $20.4 \pm 15.6$\% mid-band dwelling across full-year (365-day) cross-year evaluation (2023 training, 2024 testing) under the simple efficiency-based model. Cross-fidelity analysis shows small objective variation across battery models: 11.788\,M\textyen{} (simple EBM), 11.740\,M\textyen{} (Thevenin loss-only), 11.739\,M\textyen{} (Thevenin Rint-only), and 11.737\,M\textyen{} (Thevenin full PBM). The logged P4 evaluation records zero shield activation in the full-year six-seed audit but a nonzero inventory-teacher action gap of $0.301 \pm 0.087$, making intervention transparency an audit outcome rather than an assumed deployment property.

The 62.5\% random-seed failure rate (10 of 16~seeds produce degenerate policies with $>50\%$ infeasible actions and $<3\%$ mid-band dwelling) underscores the systemic challenge of SAC local optima in constrained sequential problems, reinforcing the necessity of multi-seed evaluation and morphology-aware selection over single-seed cost-minimization approaches.
